How Long Does It Take to Switch an RIA's Core Technology?

A CRM can move in two weeks. A portfolio system takes six to twelve months. A custodian change takes longer than either, and the firms that underestimate the gap pay for it twice.

By Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026

The parallel run was supposed to last eight weeks. In week eleven, the new portfolio system still could not reproduce the old system’s performance numbers for a single household, and the operations manager found herself explaining to the firm’s partners why the conversion they had budgeted as a summer project was now a winter project, with no end date anyone would put in writing. The software was fine. The data was the problem, and the data had been the problem since the first export.

Every RIA that switches its core technology learns the same lesson at a different price: the software is the easy part. The timeline is set by the data, the custodian feeds, and the history. A customer relationship system can move in one to two weeks. A portfolio management system takes six to twelve months for a mid-size firm. A custodian change takes longer than either, and the firms that budget for the software and forget the migration pay for the switch twice.

The calendar, system by system

Start with the easy end. Wealthbox, the CRM built for independent RIAs, says firms can migrate and deploy in one to two weeks without implementation consultants, at $59 per user per month for its basic tier, with more than 150 native integrations and direct feeds from Charles Schwab, Fidelity, and BNY Mellon Pershing. Salesforce Financial Services Cloud sits at the other end of the CRM spectrum: $225 per user per month at the enterprise tier, with large rollouts for fifty or more advisors requiring specialized administrators and third-party consultants, driving three-year total cost of ownership to four to six times the turnkey tools, or $21,000 to $187,000 and up for a ten-user team. The CRM decision is a weeks-versus-quarters decision, and the price gap is the clue.

Portfolio management is where the calendar gets serious. Orion, the platform that anchors many RIA stacks, states its goal plainly: complete onboarding, from the initial call to the transfer to service, in six months. Its history conversion alone takes 90 to 120 days to complete, plus another two to three weeks to work through the remainders in the historical data, and Orion completes one historical conversion at no cost during onboarding. A full platform subscription starts at $13,000 a year, and the company embeds Redtail CRM, which it acquired in 2021 and which supports more than 100,000 advisors, directly in its back-office engine.

The industry’s most detailed public implementation guide, published by Wealth Desk Plus in 2026, puts the full range at six to twelve months end to end for a mid-size wealth manager with a few thousand accounts and two to five custodians, and twelve to twenty-four months for a private bank with several booking centers or in-house custody. The phases overlap, but each has its own duration: scope and data mapping, four to eight weeks; custodian feed onboarding, six to sixteen weeks starting in week two; history conversion, six to twelve weeks; models, workflows, and testing, six to ten weeks; parallel run and cutover, six to twelve weeks including hypercare. Implementation services typically run at half to one-and-a-half times the first-year license fee, roughly $100,000 to $750,000 for mid-size firms and well into seven figures for enterprise programs.

Where the months actually go

The first place time disappears is cost-basis history. The guide’s recommendation is full lot-level cost basis, three to five years of transactions, and imported historical monthly returns for older periods. That is the standard that makes the new system’s performance numbers match the old one’s, and it is also the standard that breaks when the old system stored lots inconsistently, when corporate actions were never adjusted, or when the data lived partly in spreadsheets that nobody owns anymore. Missing lots and unadjusted corporate actions are the most common reason history conversions stretch past their quoted window.

The second place is custodian feeds. Feed onboarding takes six to sixteen weeks, and the delay is rarely technical. It is authorization paperwork and custodian queues: the forms that must be signed, the credentials that must be issued, the feed that must be tested against live accounts before anyone trusts it. A firm switching custodians at the same time as its software, which is common, because the Fidelity $100 million minimum taking effect June 30, 2027 is forcing exactly that decision on more than 400 firms, is running two queues at once, and the slower one sets the date.

The third place is the parallel run. The guide lists it as six to twelve weeks, and it is the phase most firms underestimate, because a parallel run that will not reconcile is not a phase. It is a project. The new system must produce the same performance, the same billing, and the same reports as the old one, and every difference has to be explained before the firm can cut over. Most go-lives slip for four reasons, the guide says: custodian feeds, cost-basis history, model mapping, and a parallel run that will not reconcile. Three of the four are data problems. The fourth is a data problem wearing a workflow costume.

“The software is the easy part. The timeline is set by the data, the custodian feeds, and the history.”

The tools that compress the calendar

The market has noticed, and the newest products are built to attack the timeline rather than the feature list. Orion embedded components of Forms Logic’s Migrator software into its onboarding process in November 2026, organizing transition data, identifying and pre-filling paperwork, automating workflows, and tracking progress in real time through a six-step sequence from advisor introduction to execution of the move. OnBord, the digital account opening platform, launched an integration with Schwab Advisor Center in November 2026 that it says can take client account opening from weeks to minutes, with compliant SMS data collection and a direct reduction in not-in-good-order errors, the back-and-forth with custodians that eats calendar time. NDEX Systems deepened its Schwab integration the same month with Data Bridge, which ingests custodial feeds and normalizes them for the firm’s existing CRM and reporting tools, and F-Engine, its wealth management platform.

None of these tools changes the fundamental arithmetic. They compress the administrative work around the migration, not the migration itself. The history still has to be reconciled, the feeds still have to be authorized, and the parallel run still has to match. What the tools do is remove the manual steps that used to sit between those milestones, and for a firm switching under a deadline, that is the difference between a six-month project and a nine-month one.

The case for staying put — and why it fails

The counterargument deserves a hearing, because switching is expensive and disruptive, and the incumbent system has one overwhelming advantage: it works. The staff knows it, the clients are used to it, and the data is already in it. A firm that stays put avoids the conversion, the parallel run, and the retraining, and for a firm whose current stack is adequate, that is a real saving. The vendors know this, which is why incumbent pricing is often the most flexible number in the negotiation right after a firm announces it is leaving.

The counterargument fails on the calendar, not the price. The costs of staying are not fixed. They rise with every custodian letter, every referral minimum change, and every integration the incumbent declines to build. Fidelity’s $100 million threshold is a deadline, not a preference, and Schwab’s referral minimum has risen from $500,000 to $2 million and then toward $5 million in eighteen months. A firm that stays on a platform whose roadmap no longer includes it is not avoiding a migration. It is postponing one, and postponement is the most expensive form of migration, because it happens on the vendor’s schedule instead of the firm’s.

How to run the switch

The practical version fits the same pattern as every other technology decision in this series: audit first, then move. Map every workflow that depends on the current system, and price the rebuild before signing anything. Test the data export before committing to the new vendor, not after, because an export that has never been tested is a hope, not a plan. Budget the parallel run as a project with its own owner and its own end date, and do not let it drift. And sequence the changes: move the CRM first, because it is fast and it proves the firm can change; move the portfolio system second, because it is slow and it needs the runway; move the custodian last, or not at all, because it is the change that touches every client.

The firms that switch best are not the ones that pick the fastest vendor. They are the ones that treat the migration as the project and the software as the tool, that budget for the data before they budget for the license, and that start the history conversion the week they decide, not the week they sign. A core technology switch takes six to twelve months because the data takes six to twelve months. The firms that accept that, and plan for it, are the ones that finish on schedule.
