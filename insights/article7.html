Custodian Consolidation: What Schwab and Fidelity's Next Moves Mean for RIA Technology

Fidelity's $100 million minimum takes effect June 30, 2027. Schwab custodies 67.8% of RIAs. How custodian decisions ripple into RIA software choices - and where firms keep leverage.

By Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, addressed to the principal of a $60 million registered investment adviser in Ohio. It was polite, formal, and final. Fidelity Investments, the firm’s custodian for nine years, would require $100 million in assets held at Fidelity by June 30, 2027, or the firm would need to move its custody elsewhere. The adviser had $60 million at Fidelity and $40 million at Schwab. On paper, the firm was a $100 million firm. At Fidelity, it was a $60 million firm, and $60 million was no longer enough.

That letter, and the hundreds like it, is the visible edge of a consolidation that has been reshaping the RIA business for six years. Custody used to be a commodity decision. It is now a strategic one, and the custodian a firm chooses increasingly decides what software it can run, what data it can move, and how much leverage it has the next time the terms change.

How two custodians became the market

The concentration starts with a single transaction. Charles Schwab acquired TD Ameritrade in 2020 in a $26 billion all-stock deal, removing the industry’s most advisor-friendly custodian as an independent alternative. The deal folded roughly $1.3 trillion in assets and 3.6 million accounts, serving more than 7,000 RIAs, into Schwab, according to Nexus Strategy’s August 2026 white paper. The integration moved more than 17 million client accounts onto Schwab’s platform and produced $2 billion in annualized cost synergies, per Schwab’s February 2026 10-K filing. Schwab now custodies for approximately 15,000 independent advisors and more than $10 trillion in total client assets.

The market-share data confirms what advisors feel. AdvizorPro’s 2026 RIA Custodian Trends Report, based on Form ADV data for 15,722 RIAs pulled October 1, 2026 and summarized by Rankite, puts Schwab as custodian to 67.8 percent of RIAs and $4.5 trillion of their separately managed account assets, nearly four times Fidelity’s share of firms. Fidelity is second by both measures: 17.4 percent of RIAs and $3.6 trillion in SMA assets. More than 65 percent of new state-registered firms chose Schwab in 2025, according to the company. Two firms now hold the custody relationship for roughly five out of six RIAs in America.

Fidelity’s $100 million rule is the other side of the same concentration. Existing firms below $100 million held at Fidelity must reach that threshold by June 30, 2027, or move custody, Rankite reported, and more than 400 SEC-registered firms report under $100 million at Fidelity. The threshold counts only assets held at Fidelity, so a $300 million firm splitting custody across two platforms can still fail it. For the firms below the line, 2026 is a transition project, not a planning exercise, and the receiving platforms, Schwab, Altruist, Betterment, TradePMR, and Axos, are competing openly for them.

Custodian as competitor

Consolidation alone would be a manageable problem. What makes it a strategic one is that the largest custodian is no longer content to be a custodian. Schwab built the Schwab Advisor Network in 2002 on a simple exchange: custody your assets with Schwab, and Schwab will send you clients. For two decades the client minimum for a referral sat at $500,000. Schwab raised it to $2 million effective January 2026, and according to an August 2026 Citywire report citing an internal Schwab memo, the firm is already moving the goalpost again, to $5 million. In roughly eighteen months, the bar for a client to qualify for a Schwab referral has risen tenfold.

The reason is not secrecy. It is strategy, stated on the record. Rick Wurster, who became Schwab’s chief executive on January 1, 2025, has told Wall Street analysts that the company intends to convert up to 31 percent of its eight million self-directed retail accounts into fee-paying advisory relationships, using its own advisors, its Schwab Wealth Advisory RIA, and an expanding force of branch-based financial consultants. Managed-account flows into Schwab’s own wealth advisory and referral programs combined rose roughly 50 percent year over year in the first half of 2026, reaching $41 billion. The custodian that once fed RIAs clients is now competing for those clients with its own advisory business, on a platform it controls.

Fidelity, meanwhile, is fighting a different battle, and it explains the $100 million threshold. Rick Wurster has also put Fidelity’s $5-trillion-plus workplace business in play, promising investment in recordkeeping technology and a “total asset capture” strategy aimed at the 401(k) market Fidelity has dominated for decades, RIABiz reported in June 2026. Fidelity holds more than 43 million plan participants against Schwab’s 5.8 million. The small RIA is caught between two strategies that were not designed with it in mind.

The challengers smell an opening

Every consolidation creates an opening, and this one has drawn well-funded challengers. Robinhood closed its acquisition of TradePMR on February 26, 2025, for approximately $300 million, acquiring a custodial and portfolio management platform with about 350 firms and more than $40 billion in assets under administration, according to Robinhood’s announcement. By the second quarter of 2026, TradePMR had reached $50 billion in assets under management and launched the Robinhood Advisor Network to refer Robinhood customers to RIAs. The buildout is slow: TradePMR chief executive Robb Baldwin has said a home-built custody and clearing system may take until 2028, and the firm extended its Wells Fargo clearing contract through 2032 in June 2026 to buy time.

Altruist, the other Silicon Valley challenger, is the fastest riser by firm count and the third-largest custodian by that measure, according to Rankite, with the fastest median AUM growth among its clients at 28.3 percent. Its chief executive, Jason Wenk, has argued publicly that building RIA custody from the ground up is extremely hard, and that the incumbents’ decades-old infrastructure is exactly the vulnerability a modern platform can exploit. Schwab’s custody head, Jon Beatty, answered the Fidelity moment with a public welcome: in a LinkedIn post on October 3, 2026, reported by RIABiz, he noted Schwab serves more than 11,000 firms under $100 million in assets, through dedicated service teams, technology, and wealth and banking services. The war for the sub-$100 million firm is being fought in public, in LinkedIn posts and conference stages, because every firm that moves is a firm that may not move again for a decade.

What consolidation does to your software

Here is where custody stops being a back-office decision and starts being a technology decision. A custodian’s platform determines which portfolio management tools integrate cleanly, which trading and rebalancing workflows run natively, and which data flows into the CRM without manual work. Schwab’s platform offers the deepest third-party integration market in the industry, which is one reason firms stay even when the referral terms worsen. Fidelity’s Wealthscape platform is well regarded for trading, workflow, and integrations, but it is now a platform for firms with $100 million at Fidelity, and a firm below that line loses access to the roadmap along with the custody.

The lock-in works through data and workflow, not through contracts. A firm that runs its rebalancing on the custodian’s native tool, its reporting on the custodian’s data feed, and its client portal on the custodian’s login has built its operating model on a counterparty that can change its minimums, its referral terms, or its pricing with a letter. Moving custody then means rebuilding the stack, retraining staff, and re-papering hundreds of households, a project that costs months and six figures. That switching cost is the custodian’s real leverage, and it grows with every integration the firm adds.

The defense is portability, and it is a technology decision before it is a negotiation tactic. Firms that keep their CRM, planning software, billing, and reporting in vendor tools that work across custodians can change custody without changing their operating model. Firms that run two custodians, splitting assets deliberately, keep a live alternative and a credible threat. The day the letter arrives, the firm with a second custodian already running has a move to make, and the firm without one has a project to start.

The case that advisors still hold the cards

The consolidation story has a counterargument, and it is worth stating plainly. Advisors are not captives. More than 65 percent of new firms chose Schwab in 2025, which means they chose it, and the firms moving off Fidelity in 2026 are choosing their next home in a competitive market, with Schwab, Altruist, TradePMR, Apex, Betterment, and Axos all bidding. Fidelity remains a strong second platform, and its decision to focus upmarket is a segmentation choice, not a market failure. A firm with $100 million at Fidelity gets a well-regarded platform and a custodian that wants its business. A firm below the line gets a deadline, but also a market full of suitors.

There is also a limit to how far the custodian-as-competitor strategy can go. Schwab’s referral program, even at a $5 million minimum, serves a small slice of its eight million retail accounts, and RIAs still own the relationship with the clients they serve. The firms that should worry are the ones whose entire growth plan was a referral program they did not control. The firms with their own growth engine, referrals, centers of influence, and a visible brand, lose little when a custodian changes its terms, because they never depended on them.

What to do before the next letter

The practical work is unglamorous and it starts now, not when a deadline arrives. Audit the stack for custodian dependence: name every workflow that breaks if the custodian changes, and price the rebuild. Keep client data in systems the firm owns, with exports tested, not assumed. Maintain a second custody relationship where the firm’s size justifies it, or at minimum a documented alternative with pricing already quoted. And treat the custodian relationship like any other vendor relationship: reviewed annually, benchmarked against alternatives, and never allowed to become the single point of failure for the whole firm.

Custodian consolidation is not finished. Schwab will keep pushing into workplace and private markets, Fidelity will keep sharpening its focus on larger firms, and the challengers will keep building. The firms that navigate it best will not be the ones that picked the right custodian. They will be the ones that built a stack that survives picking the wrong one, and kept the leverage to move when the terms change. In a market with two giants and a handful of challengers, portability is the only leverage that does not expire.
