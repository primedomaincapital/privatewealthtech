WealthTech Market 2026: The Software Powering $100T in Assets

WealthTech is an $8.9B software market steering $139T in assets. The market size, growth drivers, key players, and where the value concentrates in 2026.

By Prime Domain Capital · October 2026

One hundred thirty-nine trillion dollars in global assets under management. Roughly nine billion dollars in wealth management software revenue. The ratio between those two numbers is the entire WealthTech investment thesis.

The industry’s software bill is a rounding error against the assets it steers — and that gap is closing from both ends. Assets keep compounding, and the software capturing basis points on them keeps getting more essential. Research Nester puts the global wealthtech solutions market at $8.94 billion in 2026, growing at 15.5% annually to $37.77 billion by 2036, with North America holding around 35% share. Other tallies run hotter: Stratistics MRC counts $14.1 billion in 2026 reaching $102 billion by 2034, a 28.1% compound rate that folds in robo-advisory platforms. MarketsNXT estimates the broader wealth management technology market at approximately $31 billion in 2026, growing at over 14% a year toward $64 billion by 2031. Definitions differ; the direction does not.

What the software actually does

WealthTech is not one product. It is a stack of them, and the fastest-growing firms buy the stack, not the point solution.

At the base sits portfolio management and trading: performance reporting, account aggregation, rebalancing, model management, trade execution. Above it, financial planning software — eMoney, MoneyGuidePro, RightCapital — turns portfolio data into the goals-based plans clients actually pay for. The CRM layer, dominated by Redtail and Wealthbox, holds the relationship. Client portals and mobile access wrap the whole thing in a digital experience.

The newest layer is AI, and it has moved past the pilot phase. Windsor Drake’s 2026 analysis notes that AI copilots have reached production in mainstream advisor workflows — not as experiments, but as infrastructure. The pure robo-advisor, meanwhile, has settled into a defined segment: younger, lower-asset, digitally native investors comfortable with fully automated management. The dominant commercial model is the hybrid — a human advisor wielding automated portfolio management, planning software, and engagement tools as production infrastructure, managing more relationships at higher quality than the manual tools of the previous generation allowed.

The forces compounding growth

Four structural tailwinds are doing the compounding.

The RIA channel’s rise. The independent RIA channel now rivals the wirehouses in total assets under management, according to McKinsey. Fidelity Institutional counted 276 completed RIA transactions in 2025 across 102 unique buyers — 88% of them sponsor-backed. Every one of those deals is a technology integration project, and every newly independent advisor needs a full stack on day one.

Retail alternatives. US retail capital flowing into alternative structures reached $204 billion in 2025, up from $92 billion in 2023. Semi-liquid fund assets approached $600 billion by March 2026. Cerulli projects roughly 26,000 adviser retirements over the decade, with $3.9 trillion in assets expected to change hands. Somebody has to administer all of that complexity, and it will not be spreadsheets.

Direct indexing. Assets in directly indexed strategies are on track to exceed $800 billion by end-2026, per Windsor Drake’s analysis. Tax-loss harvesting at the individual security level is a software problem wearing an investment-strategy costume — and it is one of the clearest demonstrations that technology, not stock-picking, is now the differentiator in portfolio construction.

Regulatory clarity on digital assets. The GENIUS Act has materially accelerated institutional confidence in digital asset integration, with the OCC’s proposed rulemaking published in August 2026 giving WealthTech platforms a clear pathway for stablecoin and tokenized asset capabilities. In Europe, a consortium of major banks including ING, BNP Paribas, KBC, and UniCredit announced Qivalis in December 2025 to launch a euro-pegged stablecoin by end of 2026. Tokenized portfolios have moved from trend-deck slide to build-or-buy decision.

Where the value concentrates

A cautionary data point first: Wealthfront, the sector’s flagship listing, debuted in December 2025, closed its first day up just 1.4%, and trades 34% below its IPO price as of August 2026. Public markets remain skeptical of WealthTech multiples. Private markets are not: 43 of 44 wealthtech transactions in Windsor Drake’s exit index had a strategic acquirer. Buyer and seller expectations have converged, and recurring revenue quality — not AUM size alone — now decides the multiple.

The consolidation logic is visible at the platform layer. Envestnet announced in September 2026 that it is adding Vestmark’s trading and tax capabilities to expand its reach into larger wealth firms — an unglamorous deal that matters enormously, because every AI copilot on the market ultimately needs to sit on top of an execution layer covering unified managed accounts, tax-loss harvesting, and rebalancing in one place. The stack is compressing into fewer, deeper platforms.

Global assets under management are projected to reach $200 trillion, with total investable wealth at $481.8 trillion by 2030. The software steering those assets is a single-digit-billions market growing at mid-teens rates. That mismatch — between the value being managed and the value being captured by the tools managing it — is why capital keeps arriving.
