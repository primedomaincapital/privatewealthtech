The $300 Billion Plumbing Job: How Alternatives Became WealthTech’s Main Event

iCapital crossed $300 billion in alternatives platform assets in January 2026. CAIS closed a $170 million round at a valuation above $2 billion six months later. The pipes that move private markets into client portfolios are now the most valuable real estate in wealth technology.

By Prime Domain Capital · November 2026

The meeting was not about investments. It was about paperwork. An operations chief at a $4 billion RIA in the Midwest put a single question to her team in February 2026: a client with $2 million in a private credit fund had just received a capital call, a K-1, and a distribution notice in the same week, and nobody in the office could say, in one place, what the client actually owned. The portfolio was fine. The plumbing was not.

That gap is now the center of wealth technology. For a decade, the industry’s software race was about the front office: prettier dashboards, slicker planning tools, faster onboarding. The money has moved to the back office, to the unglamorous work of getting alternative investments — private equity, private credit, real estate, infrastructure — into ordinary client portfolios and keeping the records straight once they are there.

The numbers explain why. iCapital reported in January 2026 that its global alternatives platform had passed $300 billion in assets, $60 billion of it from wealth management clients outside the United States. Six months earlier the company had raised $820 million at a $7.5 billion valuation, led by T. Rowe Price and SurgoCap Partners, with Blackstone, KKR, Blue Owl Capital, Temasek, UBS, BNY and State Street on the cap table. The firm now works with nearly 3,900 wealth management firms and 144,000 financial professionals, and by June 2026 it was servicing roughly $1.2 trillion in assets globally.

The pipes got big

iCapital, founded in 2013 by former Goldman Sachs partner Lawrence Calcano, spent its first decade as a niche utility for advisors whose clients could meet six-figure minimums. The acquisitions of the last eighteen months show what it wants to be next. It bought Parallel Markets for reusable KYC and AML verification, took over Citi Wealth’s alternative investments feeder platform, invested in Tangible Markets to open up liquidity in private markets, and acquired LYNK Markets to distribute private alternative investment fund notes. Each deal removes one more reason an advisor would say no to an alts allocation.

Its main rival is moving on the same track. CAIS, founded in 2009, serves more than 2,500 wealth management firms and over 65,000 advisers, and in July 2026 it closed a $170 million funding round at a valuation above $2 billion, with Blue Owl Capital, Carlyle and Fortress Investment Group participating. Those three firms also sponsor funds sold through the CAIS platform — a detail that matters more than the valuation headline, and one this article returns to below.

The demand underneath both companies is no longer speculative. Mercer’s fourth annual survey, released in December 2025, found 90% of advisers already allocate to alternatives and 88% plan to increase those allocations over the next two years. Preqin, which tracks private markets fundraising and performance, estimates alternatives have grown at roughly 10% a year since 2019 and will pass $30 trillion by 2030. iCapital’s own Canada survey, conducted in October 2025, found about 92% of respondents plan to increase or maintain allocations, with roughly 48% planning to increase, and a target allocation range of 10% to 25% emerging as the new normal.

What the surveys say about the product shift

The more telling shift is inside the product mix. On iCapital’s platform, open-end funds grew from 34% of assets at the end of 2023 to 43% at the end of 2025, while closed-end structures fell from 50% to 42%. Advisors want the alts return profile without the ten-year lockup, and fund managers are obliging with evergreen structures that offer periodic liquidity. iCapital’s Asia survey, published in 2026, found the same tension from the other side: conviction in alternatives rose sharply while confidence in the economic outlook fell across every horizon measured, and risk analytics displaced transactions and CRM integrations as the top technology priority, with demand up 22 percentage points year over year.

Read together, the surveys describe an advisor who has decided alts belong in the portfolio and is now worried about the right things: how to explain a complex product to a client, how to measure its risk, and how to get out if the story changes. Complexity in explaining alternatives is now the top-ranked barrier to adoption in Asia, ahead of the client reporting problems that led the list in 2025. That is a software problem, and it is exactly the problem iCapital and CAIS sell the answer to.

The conflict nobody prices

Here is the uncomfortable part. The companies building the pipes are owned, in part, by the companies whose products flow through them. iCapital’s investors include Blackstone, KKR and Blue Owl — all sponsors of funds distributed on its platform. CAIS’s newest backers, Blue Owl, Carlyle and Fortress, sponsor funds sold through CAIS to more than 65,000 advisors overseeing roughly $8.5 trillion in client assets. InvestmentNews reported in 2026 that these conflicts became visible during a stretch of turbulence in private credit markets, when questions arose about whether advisors and end clients were adequately warned about valuation and liquidity issues in funds sponsored by iCapital’s own investors.

None of that makes the platforms unusable. It makes them intermediaries with interests, which is a different thing from the neutral plumbing they present themselves as. The fee stack tells the same story from the client’s side: a fund fee, a platform fee, and an advisory fee, each reasonable on its own, each compounding against the same dollar. The evergreen structures advisors now favor soften the liquidity problem without solving it — periodic redemption windows work until too many investors want out at once, which is precisely when they want out.

What happens next

The direction of travel is clear. Alternatives are moving from a 5% satellite allocation for wealthy clients to a 10% to 25% core holding across the mass affluent, and the firms that control the rails will set the terms on which that happens: which funds get shelf space, what data advisors see, and how fast a client can exit. The $820 million iCapital raised and the $170 million CAIS raised are not bets on a product. They are bets on distribution — on owning the shelf.

For the vendors, the prize is a toll on every alts dollar that moves through the wealth channel for the next decade. For advisors, the question is narrower and more practical: whether the platform they choose discloses whose funds it promotes, what the all-in cost is, and what happens to a client’s position when the redemption window closes. The firms that answer those questions in writing will keep the business. The ones that answer them in a footnote will eventually have to answer them in front of a regulator.
