The $3 Trillion Succession Problem: Why WealthTech M&A Is Accelerating

37% of financial advisors plan to retire within 10 years, controlling $3 trillion in assets. WealthTech consolidation is accelerating as vendors race to capture the largest wealth transfer — and advisor succession — in history.

By Prime Domain Capital · October 2026

In March 2026, a 62-year-old advisor in Scottsdale with $480 million in client assets did something his father — also an advisor — never had to do. He interviewed three technology vendors before choosing a successor. Not a junior partner. Not a rival firm. A software platform that promised to digitize his client relationships, automate his portfolio management, and make his practice sellable to a buyer he hadn’t met yet.

The advisor’s father built his book on handshakes and golf. The son is selling his on APIs and data migration.

This is the succession crisis reshaping wealth management, and WealthTech vendors are consolidating at a frantic pace to capture it.

The demographics are relentless

Cerulli Associates estimates that 37% of financial advisors — controlling approximately $3 trillion in client assets — plan to retire within the next decade. The average advisor is now 56 years old. For independent RIAs, where the founder often is the firm, succession is not a planning exercise. It is an existential event.

The technology implications are profound. A retiring advisor’s practice is worth what a buyer will pay for it, and buyers pay more for practices with clean data, documented processes, and transferable client relationships. A book built on sticky notes and memory is worth less than the same book running on a modern tech stack.

This dynamic is driving two simultaneous consolidations. First, advisory firms are merging to achieve scale — RIA M&A hit record volumes in 2025, with acquirers like Focus Financial, Hightower, and CI Financial rolling up independents. Second, the technology vendors serving those firms are merging just as fast, because the acquirers want integrated platforms, not a patchwork of point solutions.

The vendor land grab

The WealthTech M&A data tells the story. In 2025, wealth management technology saw over 120 announced acquisitions, according to Echelon Partners — up 40% from 2023. The buyers fall into three camps.

The incumbents are buying to fill gaps. Envestnet, which processes over $6 trillion in assets across its platform, acquired data aggregation capabilities to reduce its dependence on third-party providers. Orion Advisor Solutions has been rolling up planning and compliance tools to offer a unified operating system for RIAs.

The private equity firms are buying to build. Vista Equity Partners, Thoma Bravo, and TA Associates have all assembled WealthTech portfolios, betting that the independent advisor channel will continue taking share from wirehouses. PE-backed platforms can invest in R&D at a pace that bootstrapped vendors cannot match.

The wildcards are the custodians. Charles Schwab, Fidelity, and Pershing all offer proprietary technology to advisors on their platforms. As Schwab digests the TD Ameritrade integration — completed in 2024 — its technology roadmap increasingly determines what tools independent advisors actually use. A vendor that doesn’t integrate with Schwab’s platform is invisible to a huge swath of the market.

Why this matters beyond the vendors

The standard narrative frames WealthTech consolidation as a vendor story — bigger companies, better products, lower prices through scale. That narrative is incomplete.

The real consequence is standardization. As the number of independent vendors shrinks, the technology choices available to advisors narrow. An RIA that built its practice on a boutique planning tool may find that tool acquired, integrated, and eventually sunsetted. The advisor’s operational independence — the reason many went independent in the first place — erodes as their tech stack consolidates into two or three dominant platforms.

There is a countervailing force. The same succession crisis driving consolidation is also creating demand for specialized tools that the giants don’t build well — estate planning for ultra-high-net-worth families, alternative investment administration, direct indexing at small account sizes. Niche vendors serving these needs are thriving precisely because the consolidators are too large to serve them profitably.

The $3 trillion question

The advisors retiring over the next decade control $3 trillion. Their successors — whether junior partners, acquiring firms, or technology platforms — will inherit not just the assets but the operational infrastructure. The vendors that power that infrastructure will capture a share of every dollar that changes hands.

This is why WealthTech M&A is accelerating. It is not about software features. It is about positioning for the largest intergenerational transfer of client relationships in the history of financial advice. The winners will not be the vendors with the best technology. They will be the vendors embedded deepest in the practices that are about to change hands.

The Scottsdale advisor chose his platform in March. His father never had to. That difference — between a practice built on relationships and a practice built on systems — is worth billions in enterprise value across the industry. And it is the reason every WealthTech boardroom in America is running M&A scenarios right now.
