What Does a WealthTech Stack Actually Cost a $1 Billion RIA?

A $1 billion RIA's technology bill runs roughly $200,000 to $600,000 a year. The line-item budget - CRM, portfolio accounting, planning, trading, reporting - and the costs that never appear on the invoice.

By Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026

The renewal notices arrived in the same week, which is how the chief operating officer of a $1 billion registered investment adviser first saw the real number. The portfolio management platform. The CRM. The planning software. The compliance platform. The trading and rebalancing tool. The client portal. The e-signature subscription. The archiving service. Each invoice was reasonable on its own. Stacked on the desk, they added up to a figure nobody at the firm had ever calculated, because nobody had ever been asked to. The COO’s question was simple and, for an industry that sells itself on rigorous planning, oddly unasked: what does this stack actually cost?

The answer, for a $1 billion RIA, is somewhere between $200,000 and $600,000 a year, depending on how you count. The range is wide because the counting is sloppy. Most firms budget software by vendor, not by function, and the costs that matter most are the ones that never appear on a software invoice at all.

The line items

Start with the core software layer, where the numbers are best documented. TechVera’s June 2026 analysis of compliant RIA stacks puts the annual software costs for a 10-to-15-person firm in four buckets: portfolio management and performance reporting, using Orion, Tamarac, or Black Diamond, at $8,000 to $25,000 a year; CRM, using Wealthbox, Redtail, or Salesforce Financial Services Cloud, at $2,400 to $18,000 a year; financial planning software, using eMoney, MoneyGuidePro, or RightCapital, at $2,400 to $9,600 a year; and a compliance platform, using RIA in a Box, COMPLY, or Orion Compliance, at $6,000 to $20,000 a year. Add the four buckets and the core software bill for a firm of that size runs $18,800 to $72,600 a year before a single trade is placed.

Vendor-level pricing fills in the picture. LaunchAdvisor’s April 2026 startup-cost guide puts eMoney Advisor at $500 to $800 a month for an advisor license, MoneyGuidePro at approximately $1,500 to $2,500 a year, and RightCapital at $150 to $250 a month depending on tier. Redtail CRM costs $99 a month for up to 15 users regardless of client count; Wealthbox runs about $49 per user per month. Orion Advisor pricing starts around $2,500 to $5,000 a month for practices with 30 to 100 households, and Tamarac by Envestnet typically runs $10,000 to $30,000 a year depending on assets. A firm that chooses the premium option in every category pays several times what a firm choosing the value option pays, for tools that do, in most daily workflows, the same job.

The cheapest version of the stack is dramatically cheaper than the median one. FastTrackr’s April 2026 guide puts a realistic core budget for a solo advisor or two-person shop at $50 million to $100 million in assets at $4,000 to $6,000 a year: a CRM, portfolio management, planning software, and e-signatures, at roughly $340 to $440 a month. The minimum viable stack, CRM plus planning software plus e-signatures plus custodian portal access, runs under $3,000 a year.

Scaling the model to $1 billion

A $1 billion RIA is not a solo practice with a bigger number. It is typically a firm of ten to fifteen people, several hundred households, and revenue that can be estimated from the industry’s own fee data. The average advisor fee runs about 0.96 percent, according to Envestnet MoneyGuide’s 2026 State of Financial Planning Fees, as reported by R.J. Weiss in August 2026. Apply a fee near 1 percent to $1 billion in assets and the firm collects roughly $9 million to $10 million a year in revenue, before any variation for household size, service mix, or fee schedule.

Now apply the spending benchmarks. According to the 2025 Kitces Research on Advisor Technology, as cited by TechVera, the typical independent advisor spends 4 to 6 percent of annual revenue on technology, with all-in spend falling between $20,000 and $40,000 per advisor per year. For a ten-advisor firm, that is $200,000 to $400,000 a year. For the $1 billion firm on roughly $9 million to $10 million of revenue, 4 to 6 percent is $360,000 to $600,000. Kitces also found the typical advisory firm uses 12 software applications. The three methods, per-advisor, percentage-of-revenue, and vendor line items, converge on the same answer: a serious $1 billion RIA should expect a technology bill in the low-to-mid six figures, and should be suspicious of any budget far outside that range in either direction.

The per-household view is the one most firms never calculate and most need. A $1 billion firm with 400 households spending $400,000 a year on technology pays $1,000 per household per year for its stack, or about $83 a month per client, before salaries. That is the number to compare against the value each household receives: reporting, planning updates, portal access, and the rebalancing and trading work the stack performs. A firm that cannot name its cost per household is not managing its technology. It is renting it.

The costs that never appear on the invoice

Software subscriptions are the visible half of the bill. The hidden half is larger and harder to budget. Implementation is the first trap. Salesforce Financial Services Cloud, the most powerful CRM in the category, carries implementation costs of $25,000 to $100,000 or more, according to FastTrackr, a figure that can exceed a small practice’s entire annual technology budget before the first user logs in. Data migration, workflow configuration, and integration work carry similar price tags at the portfolio management layer, where a firm moving from one platform to another should expect months of dual running, two subscriptions, and staff time that no vendor quote includes.

Compliance has become a cost driver in its own right. The 2024 amendments to Regulation S-P require large RIAs, those over $1.5 billion in assets, to comply by December 2025, and smaller firms by June 3, 2026, TechVera noted in June 2026, and the SEC’s 2026 Examination Priorities specifically call out cybersecurity governance, vendor oversight, and preparedness for AI-driven intrusions. Examiners now look for evidence of actual security controls: monitoring, logging, access controls, encryption, multi-factor authentication, and incident response testing. A firm that budgeted for software and forgot security did not save money. It deferred a bill that arrives with interest.

Then there is the shelfware problem. Twelve applications, the Kitces median, means twelve renewals, twelve logins, and twelve chances to pay for a tool nobody opened in six months. The firms that overspend on technology rarely do so by choosing an expensive vendor. They do so by accumulating tools: a planning module inside the portfolio platform plus a standalone planner, a CRM with built-in workflows plus a separate workflow tool, a portal from the custodian plus a portal from the vendor. Each overlap costs a few thousand a year. Together they cost a hire.

Where the money goes to waste

The most expensive mistake is buying enterprise tools for a firm that does not need them. FastTrackr’s guide notes that enterprise-tier platforms commonly run $30,000 to $60,000 a year in licensing alone, pricing designed for firms with dedicated IT staff and more than 50 advisors. A ten-person firm that buys that tier is paying for implementation consultants, service levels, and configurability it will never use, and the vendor’s sales team will not be the one to point that out.

The second mistake is ignoring what the custodian already provides. Schwab’s Advisor Services platform includes its own iRebal rebalancing tool at no separate charge, and Fidelity’s Wealthscape platform bundles trading, workflow, and integrations for firms on its platform. A firm that pays a third-party vendor for rebalancing while its custodian offers the same function free is paying twice for one job, and the duplicate rarely shows up in any single budget line, because it sits in two different ones.

The third mistake is treating the stack as a purchase instead of a portfolio. Vendors consolidate, get acquired, raise prices, and deprecate features. Orion, Envestnet, and SS&C’s Black Diamond have all grown by acquisition, and a firm whose entire workflow depends on one vendor’s roadmap has handed its operating leverage to a counterparty. The firms that spend wisely keep their data portable and their most critical workflows in tools they could replace in a quarter if they had to.

The case for spending more

None of this is an argument for spending less. It is an argument for spending deliberately, and the counterargument deserves its weight. Technology is the cheapest leverage a $1 billion RIA can buy. A planning tool that lets one advisor serve forty households instead of twenty-five is worth far more than its subscription. A CRM that catches a client’s life event before the client mentions it is worth more than the renewal notice. The Kitces data cuts both ways: firms spending 4 to 6 percent of revenue on technology are not overspending by definition. Many of them are buying capacity, and capacity is what turns a $1 billion firm into a $2 billion firm without doubling headcount.

The firms that get this wrong in the other direction are the ones that treat technology as an expense to minimize rather than an asset to manage. They keep the $99 CRM and the spreadsheet portfolio tracker, save $50,000 a year, and lose a $5 million household to a competitor whose portal, reporting, and planning experience looked like the firm the client thought they had hired. The cheapest stack in the industry is expensive if it costs you a client.

A budget a COO can defend

The practical framework is three numbers, reviewed once a year. Cost per advisor: $20,000 to $40,000 all-in, per the Kitces benchmarks, with anything far above that requiring a written justification. Cost per household: total technology spend divided by households served, tracked year over year, with a rising number treated as a question, not a fact. And cost per function: one tool per job, with every overlap named, priced, and either eliminated or defended. A firm that can produce those three numbers in a single meeting has a technology budget. A firm that cannot has a pile of invoices.

The $1 billion RIA does not have a technology problem. It has a technology accounting problem, and the fix costs nothing: one afternoon, one spreadsheet, every subscription on one page, and the courage to cancel the tools nobody uses. A firm that knows its price can finally argue about what it is worth.
